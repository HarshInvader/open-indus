Raj (2026) reads 150 Indus signs as Old Tamil words and supports the choice of language with a held-out contest: a solver fits picture-derived word lists to half the seals so that sign pairs look like personal names, and Tamil names fit the other half far better than scrambled names (+223) while Sanskrit names do not (−13). The deposit is unusually complete, and we reproduce all 40 headline runs exactly. We then add four controls, each preregistered by hash before it was run. (1) Picture permutation. With Tamil's picture words moved onto the wrong signs at random, Tamil scores the same (median +224 over 50 permutations, p = 0.51). The contest does not depend on what the signs depict, so it cannot support readings based on what they depict. (2) Fake seals. On sign-shuffled or frequency-matched random seals the score falls to zero or below (p = 0.048, the minimum 20 fakes allow): the score tracks real sign order. (3) Object marks. The ‘copper' and ‘armlet' signs that name their objects rest on copies; counted once per distinct text, p = 0.19 and p = 0.017, neither below the paper's own multiple-test line, and random reassignment of the readings names the right object about as often (p = 0.10). (4) A wrong language with the same naming style. Japanese family names, built like Tamil names from drawable things, score +134.5 (60% of Tamil) and beat Tamil in 4 of 10 splits, but are above their control in only 7 of 10 splits, one short of our preregistered bar, so the contest narrowly passes this control. In the paper's own runs 59% of Tamil readings are sound-alike name parts rather than picture words. Further preregistered stages show that Tamil's score comes from its place, landscape and group names (people's names alone score +2.9), and that two Old Tamil name lists rebuilt by fresh AI agents from the same kinds of sources, one with the paper's own prompt, score +4 and +49; the gap lies in which names the paper's list contains. Of Tamil's sister languages, Old Malayalam scores −92, and Old Telugu and Old Kannada are void as tests. We conclude that the contest beats noise but not the placebo that matters: it measures how well a language's naming style fits the recurring sign pairs, not whether the signs write that language.
