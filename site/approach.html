Approach

We count the script as read only when ten sign sounds are tied to evidence outside the texts and predict new ones

No sign has a tested sound yet, and no outside anchor for one has been found.

Done means ten signs. Each needs a sound value backed by evidence outside the texts (a name tied to an object, a bilingual, or values fixed independently) that correctly predicts texts it was not built from, under a test sealed in advance, and survives a second team that tries to break it. Today the count is 0.

Names are the usual route. A personal name keeps its sound in any language. Whether round seals spell foreign names is now our weakest assumption.

Our name method failed its check, so we stopped it. It recovered nothing from Linear Elamite, a script read in 2022. Sound values now wait on evidence from outside the texts.

01Goal

Deciphered means ten signs with sound values anchored outside the texts

Each sound value needs an anchor outside the texts and must correctly predict texts it was not fitted to, under a test sealed in advance. Our test bench can rule a key out but cannot confirm one. It must then survive an independent red team.

02Rules

Four rules stop a good-looking result from fooling us

  1. Seal the planWritten down and fingerprinted first
  2. Build on ¾A quarter of the texts kept aside
  3. Test once on ¼No tuning on the kept texts
  4. Beat chanceAgainst shuffles, every test counted
  5. Red teamA second team tries to break it
  6. PublishWhether it holds or fails
  1. Seal the plan before the testWe write each test down first and log its SHA-256 fingerprint, a code that changes if one letter changes. The file 2026-11-14_new_finds_predictions.md was sealed on 2026-11-15 as b30497e184b142bf…
  2. Test on texts kept asideWe build on three quarters of the texts. We test once on the rest, and never tune on them.
  3. Beat chance, counting every tryWe compare each result with shuffled texts. We correct for every test the project has run, failures included.
  4. Find a planted answer firstA method must first recover the answer from a corpus of the same size where the answer is known.

Every proposed reading is scored on the same test bench

A sound key, one sound per sign, is scored on texts it was not built from, against its own sounds shuffled and keys fitted to random sign strings. None of five published keys beats its own shuffled values. Passing the bench is necessary, not enough: in our simulations a mostly wrong key often scores higher than the true one, so a high score alone proves little.

A second team tries to break every positive result

The red team reruns each positive result from scratch, looking for leaks, confounds and weak comparisons. It has reversed 37 of our results so far, each one published.

03Directions

Every starting assumption is a direction to test

We do not assume the signs record a known language. Each possibility is a direction with its own test.

  • Not language at all. Emblems or tallies. Check the traits of writing against known codes, which pass our order test too. Tested, undecided
  • A language with no relatives. Only internal structure, a bilingual or names can test it. Open
  • A known language family. Every family gets the same test, none favoured. Tested, undecided
  • Several languages or systems. Compare cities, periods and kinds of object. Partly tested
  • A mix of sign kinds. Measure the share of word, sound and number signs. Partly tested
  • Not mainly names. Texts may list goods, places or offices. Names are one route, not the base. Open
  • A partly wrong sign list. Rerun results with signs split or merged. Tested, results hold. Transcriptions differ on 1 sign in 5

04Routes

Sound values have always come from known names, but our round-seal route is now in doubt

Lost scripts have gained their first sound values from names whose sounds were already known. Round seals break normal Indus sign order at home and abroad. That fits another language, a separate seal genre or pseudo-writing, and our audit rates foreign names on them as the assumption most likely to be wrong.

On scripts we can already read, more text helped little in our first search (a stronger search did better when the model language was close), and known names help a lot. If round seals do carry names, the method needs roughly 60 to 120 round-seal texts, and about 40 are known.

Corrected 20 Nov 2026: these numbers came from a weak search. With 1,000 restarts, Latin read with an Italian model reached 62-66% of values at 10,000-30,000 signs. How much more text helps depends on how close the model language is, which is unknown for Indus.

Two finds would help most: more round-seal texts, and one seal tied to a person named in cuneiform.

05Plan

The name method failed on Linear Elamite, so we stopped it

We said in advance that a failure on a script with a known answer would end the method. It did fail, so the next step needs evidence from outside the texts.

  1. 1
    Collect every Indus-sign object found abroad Done

    55 objects. 41 have two or more readable signs, and only 9 of their readings are safe.

  2. 2
    List the names their owners could have had Done

    7,776 names from cuneiform records, and 50 Elamite names. No list of Gulf names exists.

  3. 3
    Prove the name-matching method on planted data Failed as designed

    With a long name list, the true answer scores below answers fitted to noise, even at 120 texts.

  4. 4
    Redesign it, then try it on Linear Elamite Failed, stopped

    Linear Elamite was read in 2022, so the right answer is known. Short lists and exact matches recovered none of its values: too few of its words carry a known name. As promised, the method stops here.

  5. 5
    Run it on the round seals, home and abroad Not run

    There is no point running a method that failed its known-answer check.

  6. 6
    Wait for outside evidence, with the tests frozen Now

    Requests are out for seal impressions on tablets that name men of Meluhha, museum images and excavation records. What each one could show is fixed in advance.
