01Known

What scholars agree on

A short script on small objects

The Indus civilisation flourished from about 2600 to 1900 BCE across what is now Pakistan and north-west India. Its cities included Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira. The first inscribed seal was published in 1875, and the cities themselves were excavated from the 1920s.

About 4,000 to 5,000 inscribed objects survive. Most are square stone stamp seals two to three centimetres across, usually with an animal below a line of signs. There are also clay sealings, small moulded tablets, copper tablets and marks on pottery.

The texts are very short. In Mahadevan's 1977 concordance, a face averages under four signs. Four in five have five signs or fewer, and the longest has 17.

{{FIG_SEALS}}

About 400 signs

Scholars count roughly 400 distinct signs, depending on how variants are grouped: Mahadevan lists 417, Parpola about 386. That is far too many for an alphabet or a pure syllabary, and too few for a script where every word has its own sign.

So most scholars expect a mixed, logo-syllabic system, like Sumerian cuneiform or Egyptian hieroglyphs, where some signs stand for words and others for sounds.

{{FIG_SIGNS}}

Read from right to left

Most texts run right to left. Seals were carved in mirror image so their impressions read correctly. Where a writer ran out of room, the signs crowd together at the left end. Groups of short vertical strokes are widely accepted as numbers.

Contact with Mesopotamia

Indus seals and beads turn up in Mesopotamia and around the Persian Gulf. Cuneiform texts of the same period mention trade with a land called Meluhha, which most scholars identify with the Indus region.

A few round seals in Indus style were made in the Gulf. None of these objects carries a translation.

{{FIG_MAP}}

Then it stopped

The script fell out of use as the cities declined after about 1900 BCE. No later script descends from it that we can recognise. Brahmi, the ancestor of most South Asian scripts, appears about 1,500 years later, and no link to it has been shown.

02Disputed

What is still argued over

No bilingual has been found

Egyptian hieroglyphs were read with the help of the Rosetta Stone, and Linear B with place names already known from Greek. Nothing of the kind exists for the Indus script. No object pairs Indus signs with a text in a readable script.

Which language?

The language is unknown. The most developed proposal is an early form of Dravidian, the family of Tamil and Telugu, argued by Asko Parpola and Iravatham Mahadevan. Others have proposed an Indo-Aryan language related to Sanskrit, a Munda language, or a language that has since vanished.

More than a hundred decipherments have been announced. None has been accepted by specialists.

Is it writing at all?

In 2004 Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat and Michael Witzel argued that the signs are emblems, not a record of speech. In 2009 Rajesh Rao and colleagues answered with statistics suggesting language-like structure. Critics replied that such statistics cannot tell writing from other sign systems.

Our own tests agree with the critics on that point: at this corpus size, the statistics cannot settle the question either way.
