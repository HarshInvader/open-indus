What we did this week, and what came out

  1. 27 Nov

    ResultBrahui, the Dravidian language closest to the Indus, is void on the viral AI decipherment’s contest

    A reader asked whether we tried Brahui. We have now, with the plan fixed in advance and the name list, picture words and labels built by fresh AI agents and checked against Bray’s grammar and the Dravidian Etymological Dictionary. Only 28 of the 171 pictured things have a native Brahui word, and most parts of Brahui names are borrowed from Balochi, Persian or Arabic. The paper’s code reads no signs with it (two if the borrowed parts are kept), so the test is void, like Old Telugu and Old Kannada. It says nothing for or against a Dravidian Indus language: the contest cannot test Brahui.

  2. 27 Nov

    ResultJapanese names built from the Indus sign pictures now beat the paper’s Tamil list: +235 against +223

    We gave our Japanese list built from words for the pictured things on the signs the same mix of name kinds as the paper’s Tamil list (family names, clans, gods and shrines, mountains, rivers, places) and added 398 more real names. With the paper’s code unchanged it scores +235, above the paper’s Tamil +223, above chance in all 10 runs. On seals held out from every choice it scores +128 against Tamil’s +104, and after a search tuned it on the other half of the seals, +156. Each check was fixed in advance. This is a demonstration of how far list-building moves the score, not evidence for Japanese.

  3. 27 Nov

    ResultA Japanese name list built from the Indus sign pictures scores +186 on the viral AI decipherment’s contest, close to the paper’s Tamil +223

    Deliberately and in the open, we built a list of real Japanese names of every kind that contain words for the pictured things on the signs. With the paper’s code unchanged it scores +186, and on seals held out from every choice it ties the paper’s Tamil list (+105 against +104). Picking Japanese names by score, tested on unseen seals, also reaches Tamil’s level. Picture words alone do not do it; the mix of place, mountain and shrine names does. Each check was fixed in advance. A score that list-building moves this far cannot identify the seals’ language.

01The script

The writing of a vast early civilisation is still unread

About four thousand short texts survive from the Indus cities, 2600 to 1900 BCE, most on stone seals.

The cities rivalled those of Egypt and Mesopotamia in size and planning, yet left nothing we can read.

So we do not know their language, what they called their cities, or who ruled them. The script is the only direct evidence.

03Found so far

What we have found so far

  • There are rules. The signs appear in a fixed order, the way words do in a sentence. So these are not random marks.
  • Some seals don’t follow them. A few round seals, mostly found around the Persian Gulf, break the usual order. They might be written in a foreign language.
  • No one has cracked it. Many people have claimed to read the script. We tested five of those claims, and none did better than guessing.

04How we work

How we try to read it

Nobody can read these signs yet. We look for patterns, make a guess about what the signs mean, and check it on texts we kept hidden, so we cannot fool ourselves.
