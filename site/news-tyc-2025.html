Preprint · Mar 2025

Do Indus signs look more like scripts from the Himalayas than from Mesopotamia?

ivc2tyc project · arXiv 2503.21074

In a sentence

An image-AI comparison claims Tibetan-Yi corridor scripts resemble Indus signs most.

In plain words

Using image AI, the authors compare sign shapes and report that scripts from the Tibetan-Yi corridor, such as Naxi Dongba and Yi, look more like Indus signs than proto-cuneiform or Proto-Elamite do. Looking alike does not mean sounding alike, but their open sign images are useful.

Technical detail

Hybrid vision embeddings over glyph image sets for Indus, proto-cuneiform, Proto-Elamite and five Tibetan-Yi scripts (GitHub oohalakkadi/ivc2tyc, MIT; recommended by PCI Archaeology). Shape similarity carries no sound values: Linear Elamite look-alikes failed blind (p = 0.83).

How we read it

Read: full text. Sources opened: arxiv.org/pdf/2503.21074.

What they did

  • The author compared sign images from the Indus script, proto-cuneiform and proto-Elamite with signs from newer Dongba, old Naxi and a mixed Tibetan-Yi corridor set of Ba-Shu, Classical Yi and old Naxi signs (section 3.1).
  • She trained contrastive CNN-Swin image encoders with an NT-Xent loss at temperature 0.1 (section 3.5).
  • For each target script she trained five models, fifteen in all, each in its own embedding space (section 3.4, Table 3.1).
  • She compared glyph-level and script-level cosine similarity between the target scripts and the corridor set.

What they found

  • Mean glyph-level cosine similarity with the corridor set is 0.635 for Indus, 0.102 for proto-cuneiform and 0.078 for proto-Elamite (Table 4.1, p. 31).
  • Script-level similarity is 0.930 for Indus (interval 0.917 to 0.942), 0.887 for proto-cuneiform and 0.855 for proto-Elamite (section 4.1.1).
  • Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) are 7.13 to 8.36, which the author says are inflated by low variance (Table 4.3; section 5.1.1).
  • Several ensemble members stopped at epoch 1, and validation losses lie roughly between 8.7 and 10.2 (Table 3.1, pp. 23-24).
  • The author lists independent origin, common ancestry and convergent evolution as alternative explanations (section 5.4).

Strengths and limits

  • The code is public (github.com/oohalakkadi/ivc2tyc, section 6.3) and the author is open about inflated effect sizes.
  • Similarities from separate embedding spaces, trained per target script, cannot be compared across target scripts.
  • Several models barely trained, so the embeddings may reflect image style more than sign form.
  • Our own red-team run found the Indus advantage disappears when the Ba-Shu signs are removed (experiments/redteam_ivc2tyc).

What the authors say comes next

  • Study Tibetan rock art and archaeology along the Shu-Shendu road. (Section 6.2, p. 43)
  • Add linguistic and semantic constraints to the embeddings and include more Central and East Asian scripts as controls. (Section 6.2, p. 43)

Ideas it gives us

  • Retrain one shared embedding space on all scripts together, then compare Indus-to-corridor similarity against a null of shuffled script labels and against unrelated control scripts drawn in a similar style. This removes the cross-space comparison that makes the published numbers incomparable. Open data enough: no. Needs: The ivc2tyc code and sign images (public), plus open sign images for several unrelated control scripts.
  • Report the corridor similarity sign by sign and check whether the Indus advantage comes from a handful of simple shapes such as strokes, circles and crosses. Simple geometric signs recur in unrelated scripts and could drive the result alone. Open data enough: yes. Needs: The ivc2tyc repository outputs and our existing red-team run.

What we take from it

Tested

We red-teamed it. The claim technically survives, but its whole edge comes from one small script, Ba-Shu. Proto-cuneiform is closer to Indus than four of the five Himalayan scripts.

The idea we are borrowing

Test the claim that Indus signs look most like Himalayan scripts
