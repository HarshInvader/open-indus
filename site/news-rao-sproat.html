Debate · 2009–2014

Does sign order prove it is a language? The numbers say no

Rao et al. 2009 (Science); Sproat 2010, 2014 (Language) · Science 324; Language 90:457

In a sentence

A measure of how predictable the next sign is put Indus near languages. Critics showed non-writing scores there too.

In plain words

Rao’s team measured how predictable the next sign is and found the Indus script sits in the same band as languages. Sproat replied that symbol systems that are not writing can score the same way, and that two better measures put Indus with non-writing. Later work, including SIGIL, settled it: these numbers alone cannot decide.

Technical detail

Conditional entropy of sign bigrams (Rao et al. 2009); n-gram restoration of missing signs (Yadav et al. 2010, PLoS ONE). Sproat (2010, 2014): the entropy band does not separate writing from non-writing; two measures that do put Indus and Pictish on the non-linguistic side.

How we read it

Read: partial (full text of Rao et al. 2009 Science with its supplement, Sproat 2010 ‘Last Words’ in Computational Linguistics, and the Rao et al. 2010 reply; Sproat 2014 Language article is not open access and MUSE showed a bot check, so only its abstract on Cambridge Core and its open online supplement were read). Sources opened: homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/ScienceIndus.pdf, aclanthology.org/J10-3012.pdf, homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/IndusCompLing.pdf, www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/abs/statistical-compa, static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S00978507.

What they did

  • Rao and colleagues computed the conditional entropy of the next sign given the previous one, with Kneser-Ney smoothing, for the Indus texts and for several languages, DNA, protein, Fortran and two artificial ‘non-linguistic’ systems.
  • Their two non-linguistic controls were generated by the authors: one with every sign equally likely to follow any other, one with each sign having a single fixed successor.
  • Sproat (2010) replied that those two controls describe no real symbol system, and showed that a random corpus with Zipf-like sign frequencies and no sequential structure reproduces the Indus curve.
  • Sproat also tested the Pictish entropy classifier of Lee, Jonathan and Ziman on Mesopotamian kudurru deity symbols and on dice rolls.
  • Rao et al. (2010) answered that they never claimed entropy alone proves language, extended the analysis to block entropies up to six signs, and framed the question as a Bayesian weight of evidence.
  • Sproat (2014) built corpora of seven non-linguistic and fourteen linguistic systems and tested all proposed measures on them.

What they found

  • Rao et al. used 1,548 lines and 7,000 sign occurrences from Mahadevan 1977, dropping texts with doubtful signs, multi-line texts and duplicates (Supplement, Datasets).
  • Indus conditional entropy sat in the band of the language corpora, closest to Sumerian and Old Tamil, and far from the two artificial controls (Fig. 1).
  • Indus perplexity drops from 68.82 for single signs to 26.69 for bigrams and only to 25.26 by n = 4-5, so most predictable structure is captured by sign pairs (Supplement, Table S1).
  • Sproat reports that a 400-element Zipf distribution with alpha = 1.5 and independent bigrams fits the Indus result well (Sproat 2010, section 2, p. 589).
  • On the Lee et al. measures, a kudurru corpus of 545 tokens and 59 types scores Cr = 8.0 and Ur = 1.55, and 75 dice-roll ‘texts’ of 638 symbols score Cr = 12.64 and Ur = 1.18, so both are classed as writing (pp. 591-592).
  • Sproat 2014 abstract: none of the previously published methods is useful as published; one Lee et al. measure with a different cut-off and a new repetition-based measure do separate the two classes, and both put Indus and Pictish on the non-linguistic side (abstract only).

Strengths and limits

  • Sproat’s core criticism holds: Rao’s non-linguistic controls were synthetic extremes, and a frequency-matched random baseline lands in the language band.
  • Rao’s narrower point, that Indus sign order is far from random and far from rigid, is true but was already known.
  • Sproat 2014’s positive result rests on a small number of systems and on cut-offs fitted to them; with about 20 labelled systems a classifier can easily overfit, and the project’s own calibration (language_vs_code) found no metric reliable at Indus size.
  • Both sides treat the Indus corpus as one genre; mixing seals, tablets and pots may move any statistic.

What the authors say comes next

  • Infer an underlying grammar for the Indus texts and compare the inferred rules with those of candidate language families such as Dravidian and Indo-European. (Rao et al. 2009, Supplement, ‘Affinity of Indus texts with Dravidian versus Indo-European’)
  • Cryptography-style language recognition tests may be worth exploring for undeciphered scripts. (Rao et al. 2009, Supplement, ‘Relationship to cryptographic techniques’)
  • Assemble a large sample of known linguistic and non-linguistic symbol systems and show a measure classifies them with high accuracy before applying it to Indus or Pictish. (Sproat 2010, end of section 2)
  • General science journals should include computational linguists as reviewers of such papers. (Sproat 2010, section 3)
  • Further non-linguistic corpora were under development beyond the seven released. (Sproat 2014 supplement, section 3)

Ideas it gives us

  • Run the same entropy and repetition measures separately on Indus genres (square seals, bar seals, moulded tablets, incised tablets, pots), each against a genre-matched null. The whole debate treats Indus as one corpus; if genres differ strongly, some apparent ‘language-like’ or ‘code-like’ signal is a mixture effect. Open data enough: yes. Needs: ICIT object type and M77 texts; the language_vs_code metric code.
  • Add Sproat’s released non-linguistic corpora (kudurrus, totem poles, barn stars and others) to the project’s calibration set, if the files are still online and licensed for reuse. The calibration set had only six codes; more real codes would make the posterior sharper or show it cannot move. Open data enough: no. Needs: The rws.xoba.com nonlinguistic-symbols files named in the Sproat 2014 supplement; availability and licence not checked.
  • Reproduce Sproat 2014’s repetition measure on Indus with the project’s family-level deduplication and a SIGIL-style emblem generator as a known non-linguistic control. Sproat’s measure was not checked against copies and moulded duplicates, which inflate or deflate repetition; it is the one measure with a published positive claim. Open data enough: no. Needs: The exact measure definition from the paywalled Sproat 2014 article (not read here).

What we take from it

Closed

Treat entropy, Zipf and n-gram arguments as closed in both directions.
